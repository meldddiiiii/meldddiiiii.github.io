PECVD calibration and deposition-rate drift analysis

What I did

I deposited SiO₂ films to a 300 nm target following the lab's full PECVD traveler: cleave p-type Si into 10 × 10 mm coupons, solvent clean (acetone, IPA, N₂), run a 20-minute chamber clean, deposit, then verify thickness and uniformity with Filmetrics and Dektak. My Summer 2026 runs came in at 0.84 to 0.88 nm/s, and I logged each one in the lab's calibration log and NEMO records so other users could plan accurate depositions.

I then went back through the full shared log and treated it as a process-control dataset: one rate per run (thickness ÷ deposition time ), plotted against date and recipe.

Findings

  1. The SiO₂ rate jumped 26%, from 0.680 to 0.855 nm/s, in late January 2026. The jump lines up exactly with the switch from the "translated" recipe to the Standard recipe, so it is a recipe change rather than slow tool drift. Within the Standard recipe the rate holds to 2.2% (1σ) over five months.
  2. The time calculator lagged the tool. It still assumed 0.82 nm/s, so 365 s runs came out at 315.7 nm on average, 5.2% over target (n = 11). Once the time was cut to 345 s, five runs averaged 296.0 nm, 1.3% under.
  3. The log had data-integrity bugs. From June 2026, ellipsometer thicknesses were entered in Å in a column labeled nm (3,524 entered where 352.4 nm was meant), so any formula reading them is off by 10×. The "ellipsometer" SiO₂ rate column actually pulls from the F20 cells, so it silently repeats the F20 numbers. Sample IDs S39 to S51 were also reused for different runs.

Next experiment

The recipe sheet lists 0.87 nm/s on 10 mm coupons but 0.826 nm/s on 3-inch wafers, about 5% lower. I'd map thickness across a full 3-inch wafer with the Dektak to see whether that gap is edge-to-center nonuniformity, then set deposition time by substrate size. On the sheet side: put units in every header, add a validation rule that rejects thickness entries above 1000, and make lot IDs unique.

Exposure dose matrix and overlay alignment

Dose test

Set up an exposure job by entering substrate type and size, die size, and the row and column count. On test coupons I ran a series/matrix job so each die received a different laser power, developed, and read off which die resolved cleanly. That set 66 mW for Layer 1.

The first Layer 1 exposure on an SiO₂ coupon failed, so I went back and ran dose tests on SiO₂ itself with both AZ1512 and AZ1518 before exposing real samples. The next real sample passed at 66 mW.

Overlay alignment

Located existing alignment marks at the pattern corners with the tool camera, entered their design coordinates, then clicked the same four points on the real substrate. The tool fits a shift and rotation between the two point sets and corrects the whole exposure to match the sample.

Tool habits I learned: plastic tweezers only, vacuum-hold the substrate, and set the z-limit just above substrate thickness, since it caps how far the write head travels and a wrong value crashes the head into the wafer.

Next experiment

Add a focus axis to the dose matrix (dose × focus) and measure critical dimension per die on a profilometer or SEM instead of a pass/fail read, so the process window has numbers on both sides.

Reflow DOE for connector and flip-chip attachment

Solder bridging between fine-pitch pads was the main failure when attaching an Omnetics connector and a flip-chip electrode array to small interposer boards. I ran a design of experiments to eliminate it. Each experimental unit was one connector on one board, scored pass or fail, and I changed one factor at a time against a fixed baseline, which took connector yield from about 80% to 99%.

Next experiment

One-factor-at-a-time misses interactions, such as flux amount mattering more at lower temperature. A 24−1 fractional factorial covers all four factors in 8 runs and separates main effects from two-way interactions. I'd also score each pin rather than each connector, which turns one pass/fail into dozens of data points per board.

04 · RELIABILITY
WhereStealth-mode neurotech startup
DevicePolymer microelectrode arrays, 2 designs
MediumPBS (phosphate-buffered saline)

Accelerated soak testing of electrode arrays

Method

Arrays from two designs were held in PBS, which mimics the salt content of body fluid, at elevated temperature to speed up aging. I tracked impedance magnitude and phase with EIS at log-spaced intervals: 4 hours, then days 1, 4, 6, 11, 12, 18, 25, 32, 36 and 43, with more points early when changes are fastest. An electrode fails when its impedance falls below 50% of its starting value: a drop means saline has found a leakage path through the insulation.

AF = 2^((T_soak − 37 °C) / 10) · equivalent days at 37 °C = soak days × AF

Each 10 °C above body temperature roughly doubles the rate of the reactions that degrade the insulation (the Q10 = 2 convention used in ASTM F1980). So a soak 20 °C above body temperature ages the arrays about 4× faster.

Next experiment

Run the same arrays at two or three soak temperatures. The failure time at each temperature lets you fit the acceleration factor directly instead of assuming Q10 = 2. Cross-section failed arrays to tell delamination from water uptake through the insulation.

ToolKiCad

Single Langmuir probe sweep and current-sense board

A plasma diagnostic board. A function-generator input drives an OPA454 high-voltage op-amp stage that sweeps the probe bias. Probe current flows through a 100 Ω shunt, and an INA149 high-common-mode difference amplifier reads the voltage across it for the DAQ. ±12 V rails come from Zener regulators (1N4742A) with local decoupling.

Two design problems: I kept the OPA454 inside its 50 mA output limit by scaling the feedback divider up 1000× and adding thermal vias for about 2 W of dissipation. The INA149 lets the DAQ read probe current at about 35 V common mode, well outside the DAQ's ±10.4 V input range.

It ties back to process work: plasma density and electron temperature are what set deposition and etch behavior in tools like the PECVD above.
